Walking Tour Script, Quay Street to the New Wall

This is the script volunteers use when they lead the Saturday tour. It takes about ninety minutes and finishes at the tea room. Please keep the stops in this order, because it follows the tide and the wind.

Stop one is the Customs Shed on Quay Street, the oldest standing building in Saltmarsh and now the society's reading room. Stand with your back to the river so people can see the iron rings in the wall where the boats were tied up. Explain that the building has been a customs post, a fish market and a bicycle shop, and that the society still argues about whether it dates from 1814 or 1819. Let people look at the cannery whistle on the shelf inside, but do not let anyone blow it.

Stop two is the Pell River slipway, two minutes along the quay. This is where Edmund Tarrow first beached his boats before he bought the flats, and it is the best place to talk about the founding of the town. Mention Honora Tarrow's ledgers, and that the society keeps them in the cabinet by the window of the reading room. If the tide is out, point out the line of old oyster shells in the mud, which marks the original landing.

Stop three is the church of St Brannoc, at the top of Bell Lane. Tell the story of the night of 14 March 1887, when Alma Voss climbed the tower and rang the bell until dawn. The story is firmly established, but the detail that a boy took over for an hour is only a family tradition, so say so. Visitors like to climb the tower, which costs two pounds and goes to the roof fund; there are ninety-one steps.

Stop four is the Marsh Gardens, down the hill from the church. These are the low streets that were never rebuilt after the flood. Point out the allotment sheds and the boathouse at the far end. Remind people that the soil is still salty and that tomatoes do oddly well here.

Stop five is the New Wall, finished in 1891, where the tour turns to the cannery years. Stand facing the car park and ask people to imagine three hundred and forty workers walking to the gate at seven in the morning. The Pell River Cannery stood where the cars are. Tell them about Dorothea Quillfeather and the closure in 1958, and do not rush this stop, because it is the one visitors remember. Then walk them to the tea room, which has been run since 1998 by the Okonkwo-Hale family and is not, strictly speaking, historic, but does a very good scone.

A note on accessibility: stops one to three are step-free, the Marsh Gardens path is uneven after rain, and the New Wall has a ramp at the south end.
