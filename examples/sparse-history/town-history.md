# A Short History of Saltmarsh

This is the working draft of the history the society has been meaning to write for years. It mixes everything together on purpose, because that is how it was written down.

## The founding

Saltmarsh began as a landing place for oyster boats in the 1790s, and for the first fifty years it had no church, no school and no street names. The man usually credited with turning the landing into a town is Edmund Tarrow, a ship's carpenter who bought the tidal flats at the mouth of the Pell River in 1811 and began selling plots to other boat builders. Tarrow's wife, Honora, kept the accounts and is the reason we know anything about the early years: her ledgers, now held in the society's cabinet, record every plot sold, every debt forgiven and, in the margins, a good deal of gossip about the neighbours.

By 1830 about two hundred people lived on the flats. The first permanent building was the Customs Shed on Quay Street, which later became a fish market, then a bicycle shop, and is now the society's reading room. The Customs Shed is the oldest standing structure in town and the first stop on every walking tour.

## The flood of 1887

The event every resident of Saltmarsh knows about is the flood of 1887. A spring tide, a gale from the south-east and a week of rain in the hills combined to push the Pell River over the sea wall on the night of 14 March. The water reached the second step of the Customs Shed and, in the low streets behind it, stood a full metre deep in the front rooms. Nobody drowned, which people put down to the bell-ringer Alma Voss, who climbed the tower of St Brannoc's church at midnight and rang until dawn, waking the streets in time for families to carry children and valuables upstairs.

The flood changed the town in three lasting ways. The sea wall was rebuilt higher and in stone, finished in 1891 and still called the New Wall by anyone over seventy. The low streets were never built on again and became the Marsh Gardens. And the Flood Relief Committee, formed that spring to share out donations from neighbouring towns, did not disband when the money ran out. It turned into the first town council, with Alma Voss's brother Silas as its first chairman.

## The cannery years

Between 1902 and 1958 the largest employer in Saltmarsh was the Pell River Cannery, which stood on the north bank where the car park is now. At its peak it employed three hundred and forty people, most of them women, and shipped tinned mackerel and crab to inland markets by rail. The cannery was run for most of its life by Dorothea Quillfeather, who took it over from her father in 1924 and kept it open through the lean years by switching to tinned vegetables during the war. When it finally closed, the town lost nearly a third of its jobs in a single season, and the population fell by a quarter over the following decade.

The cannery's whistle is preserved at the reading room. Older residents say they still time their lunch by it, although it has not sounded in sixty years.

## Open questions

The society has never settled when the Customs Shed was actually built; Honora Tarrow's ledger mentions it in 1819, but a survey map suggests 1814. We also do not know what became of Edmund Tarrow after 1836, when the ledger simply stops mentioning him.
